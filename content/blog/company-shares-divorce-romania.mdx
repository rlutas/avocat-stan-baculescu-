*During the marriage, one spouse sets up a limited liability company (SRL) that grows and turns a steady profit. In a later property division, one question becomes unavoidable: who actually owns the company?*

The answer surprises people who believe that a deed signed by one spouse automatically means sole ownership. In reality, the entry in the Trade Register only shows who is a shareholder. Under the matrimonial property regime, what matters is where the money came from when the company was set up.

<KeyPoints>
- Shares acquired during marriage with joint money or assets are joint property, even if only one spouse is listed as shareholder
- If the contribution came from separate property (an inheritance, a gift, money owned before marriage), the shares remain separate property
- In the division, the other spouse usually receives an equalisation payment in money, not shareholder status
- The company's value is set by a court-appointed expert, not by the share capital in the articles of association
</KeyPoints>

## The myth: "the company is mine, I founded it"

This is one of the most common misconceptions we see in practice. The fact that only one spouse appears as sole shareholder in the articles of association says nothing about the legal status of the shares. What really matters is when the company was set up and where the contribution to the share capital came from.

## Joint ownership does not stop at the company's door

<LegalCitation source="Art. 339, Romanian Civil Code">
Property acquired by either spouse during the legal community regime is, from the date of acquisition, joint property of the spouses.
</LegalCitation>

If the company was set up during the marriage and the contribution came from joint income or assets, the shares are joint property, even if the company documents name only one spouse.

There are exceptions. **If the contribution came from separate property** (an inheritance, a gift or money acquired before the marriage), the resulting shares remain separate property and fall outside the division.

<Callout type="warning" title="Who has to prove it">
Joint ownership is a rebuttable presumption. The spouse who claims the company is "only theirs" must prove that the contribution to the share capital came from separate property.
</Callout>

## What "getting a share of the company" really means

This is the distinction that changes how many people see a property division: being entitled to a portion of the company does not automatically make you a shareholder.

- **Dividing the shares in kind** means amending the articles of association so that the other spouse becomes a shareholder. This is only possible under the strict conditions of Company Law no. 31/1990 and any clauses in the company's articles.
- **Dividing the economic value of the shares** is the solution most often seen in practice.

The reasoning is simple: a business cannot run efficiently with an imposed shareholder who has never been involved in managing it. The spouse who ran the company usually keeps their shareholding, and the other spouse receives a cash equivalent, called *sultă* in Romanian law, in proportion to their share. Under art. 357 of the Civil Code, the spouses' shares are presumed equal unless proven otherwise.

## What a company is actually worth in a division

One important detail: the share capital is not an indicator of the company's value. It is only an accounting requirement and is usually set at a symbolic 200 lei, an amount unrelated to the company's real market value.

The real value is set by a court-appointed expert at the date of the division, taking into account:

1. **Net book assets**, the difference between everything the company owns and everything it owes.
2. **The real market value** of the company's assets (property, equipment, stock), where the accounting records no longer reflect reality.
3. **Retained earnings**, which increase the company's real worth even if they have not yet been paid out to shareholders.
4. **Any artificial reduction of assets**, a manoeuvre the courts check closely when there are signs of it.

<Callout type="info" title="In short">
A company set up during the marriage with joint resources is part of the property division even if only one spouse is a shareholder. What matters is not who remains a shareholder, but what the other spouse's share is worth. The right answer almost always comes from a rigorous valuation, not from an assumption.
</Callout>
